# On Science

This essay examines what distinguishes a scientific discipline by shifting the focus from the “scientific method” to the nature of scientific explanation, drawing on Carl Hempel’s nomological–deductive model and his notion of explanation sketches. I argue that sciences are differentiated by the phenomena they target, the tools they use to search for lawful structure, and the languages in which they express explanations. Within this framework, artificial intelligence is situated as a science of substrate-free intellectual processes that combines deductive theoretical results with largely statistical explanatory practices. The essay concludes that AI is scientific not by method alone, but by its systematic attempt to discover and formalize the laws of intelligence.

---

## What Makes a Discipline a Science? Reflections from Philosophy to Artificial Intelligence

When we casually refer to a field as a *science*, we often invoke a familiar phrase: *the scientific method*. The implication is that if a discipline follows a certain procedural recipe—hypotheses, experiments, data, conclusions—then it qualifies as scientific.

This view is widespread, but it is also historically shallow.

Many activities can follow something resembling the scientific method without pursuing any deep explanatory goals at all. Market research, quality control, A/B testing in industry, or even systematic trial-and-error in engineering can all look methodologically “scientific” while remaining fundamentally pragmatic and instrumental.

For philosophers and historians of science, the central question has therefore never been just *how* we investigate, but *what kind of explanations* we ultimately produce.

In this essay, I want to sketch a classical philosophical perspective on scientific explanation, and then use it to situate artificial intelligence as a scientific discipline.

---

## From Method to Explanation

Over the last century, philosophers of science have converged on a key insight:

> What distinguishes science is not primarily a method, but the kind of explanations it aims to deliver.

On this view, a discipline is scientific if it attempts to provide *scientific explanations* of a class of phenomena.

But this only pushes the problem one level deeper.

What counts as a scientific explanation?

---

## Hempel and the Nomological–Deductive Model

One of the most influential answers comes from Carl Hempel, a central figure in twentieth-century philosophy of science.

Hempel developed what is known as the **nomological–deductive model** of explanation. The word *nomological* comes from the Greek *nomos*, meaning *law*.

In its ideal form, a scientific explanation has the following structure:

- We start with one or more **universal laws**.
- We add **initial or boundary conditions**.
- We then *deduce* the phenomenon to be explained as a logical consequence.

In Hempel’s own formulation:

> An explanation is scientific in nature if it frames an observation under question as an instance of a universal law.

Classic examples come from physics:

- Kepler’s laws and Newtonian mechanics explain planetary motion.
- The gas laws explain macroscopic thermodynamic behavior.
- Maxwell’s equations explain electromagnetic phenomena.

Here, explanation is tightly linked to deduction from general laws.

---

### Explanation Sketches and Statistical Generalization

However, Hempel himself was well aware that most successful sciences do *not* operate in this idealized way.

In biology, psychology, medicine, economics, and much of the social sciences, we rarely possess strict universal laws. Instead, we rely on:

- Statistical regularities  
- Probabilistic models  
- Empirical generalizations from experience  

These do not deduce individual events with certainty. They provide likelihoods, tendencies, and patterns.

Hempel referred to these as **explanation sketches**: partial, incomplete approximations to the nomological–deductive ideal.

They are explanatory in practice, but they fall short of full law-based deduction.

This already suggests an important conclusion:

> Being a science does not require possessing universal laws.  
> It requires systematically *searching* for lawful structure in a domain.

---

## What Differentiates Scientific Disciplines?

If many fields of science share this common goal to search for scientific explanations, what differentiates them?

Three dimensions are especially important:

1. **The class of phenomena they attempt to explain**  
2. **The tools they use to search for laws or regularities**  
3. **The language in which explanations are expressed**

Physics, chemistry, biology, etc. differ not just in subject matter, but in:

- What counts as an admissible observation  
- What kinds of abstractions are allowed and how are they formulated
- What formal, symbolic and conceptual languages are used to describe explanations

With this in place, we can finally turn to artificial intelligence.

---

## AI as a Scientific Discipline

Artificial intelligence occupies an unusual position among the sciences.

On the one hand, parts of AI produce **genuinely nomological–deductive explanations**:

- Convergence theorems in optimization  
- Generalization bounds in learning theory  
- Computational complexity results  
- Formal properties of algorithms and architectures  

These are deductive, law-like, and fully within Hempel’s ideal.

On the other hand, much of modern AI relies on **statistical explanation sketches**:

- Empirical scaling laws  
- Performance trends in large models  
- Heuristic architectural principles  
- Observed regularities in representation learning  

These generalize from experience but often lack deep theoretical grounding.

AI therefore straddles both sides of Hempel’s divide.

---

### The Phenomena of AI: Substrate-Free Intelligence

What, exactly, is the class of phenomena that AI attempts to explain?

Not physical motion, chemical reactions, or biological metabolism.

AI is concerned with what might be called:

> **Substrate-free cognitive processes**

That is:

- Reasoning  
- Learning  
- Perception  
- Memory  
- Planning  
- Language  
- Decision-making  

studied independently of any particular biological or physical implementation.

This is a profound shift.

Physics abstracts by moving through levels of physical description (particles →  fields →  symmetries), but it still traffics in laws whose scope is fixed by physical ontology. It generalizes across instances of the same kind of physical system.

AI abstracts in a different direction: it treats intelligence as an organizational property of systems (regularities in computation and representation) so that the same functional patterns can, in principle, be realized across very different substrates (brains, silicon chips, cellular automata), provided they implement the relevant state transitions and exhibit sufficient representational fidelity. AI targets behaviors that are multiply realizable: it studies which computational state-transition structures and representational mappings give rise to "intelligent" patterns, in any physical system capable of implementing those structures.

Thus, while Physics seeks substrate-specific laws of nature; AI seeks substrate-independent laws of organization (computation + representation) that make certain behaviors possible.

---

### The Language of Explanation in AI

The explanatory language of AI is itself a hybrid, drawn from several mature sciences.

Today, most theoretical and empirical explanations in AI are expressed in terms of:

#### Geometry and Topology of Representation Spaces

- High-dimensional real vector spaces  
- Submanifolds induced by learned representations  
- Linear separability and curvature  
- Topological and geometric structure of embeddings  

This geometric viewpoint underlies modern representation learning.

#### Information Theory and Statistics

- Entropy, mutual information, likelihood  
- Generalization bounds  
- Bayesian inference  
- Probabilistic modeling  

These provide the dominant framework for learning and uncertainty.

#### Tensor Computation and Linear Algebra

- Multilinear maps  
- Spectral properties  
- Optimization in parameter space  
- Backpropagation as structured tensor calculus  

This is the computational substrate of modern deep learning.

Together, these form the conceptual language in which AI explanations are written.

---

## Conclusion: Science Without Laws?

If we take Hempel seriously, most of AI today produces explanation sketches rather than full nomological–deductive explanations.

But this is not a defect.

It places AI squarely in the same category as biology, medicine, and much of psychology: sciences that are *in the process* of discovering their laws.

AI is a science not because it follows a method, but because:

- It targets a well-defined class of phenomena  
- It systematically searches for lawful structure  
- It expresses its explanations in formal, cumulative languages  

Whether AI will one day discover deep, universal laws of intelligence remains open.

But the scientific character of the enterprise is already clear.

It is the attempt to explain intelligence itself.
